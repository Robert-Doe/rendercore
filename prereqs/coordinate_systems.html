Coordinate Systems & the Box Idea

Every element on a page is a rectangle at a specific position. "Position" always means relative to something — never floating in the void.

🍽️ The Restaurant

"Put the garnish 2 inches from the left" only means something once you say left of what — the plate? the table? the whole dining room? Every plating instruction in a real kitchen is relative to a specific reference frame, and a garnish 2 inches from the plate's edge lands somewhere completely different depending on where the plate itself sits on the table.

The Core Idea

Screens use a coordinate system where (0, 0) is the top-left corner, x increases rightward, and — unlike math class — y increases downward. Every element's position is a pair of numbers in this system, but almost never stated in absolute screen terms — it's computed relative to its parent's box. A child positioned at "10px from its parent's left edge" lands in a different absolute screen spot depending on where the parent itself landed — which is why layout has to work top-down: you cannot place a child correctly until you know where its parent's box begins.

Every element is treated as a rectangle — even irregularly-shaped content like wrapped text ends up inside an invisible bounding rectangle. That single simplification (everything is a box) is what makes the box model (Module 10) possible at all.

A Worked Example

parent box:  x=50, y=50, width=300, height=200

child box (declared as "10px from parent's left/top, 100x40"):
  absolute x = parent.x + 10 = 60
  absolute y = parent.y + 10 = 60
  width = 100, height = 40

Notice the child's own numbers (10, 10, 100, 40) never change — but its absolute position on screen depends entirely on where the parent box was placed. Move the parent, and every descendant moves with it without their own numbers changing at all. This is exactly the mechanism Modules 11–13 implement.

Where This Shows Up In The Course

  • Module 10 — defines what a "box" even consists of (content, padding, border, margin) before any positioning happens.
  • Modules 11–13 — compute each box's position relative to its parent, recursively, top of the tree down.
  • Module 14 — the display list stores absolute, already-resolved coordinates — by paint time, "relative to what" has already been fully resolved.
🧠 Quick check
Why does y increasing downward (instead of upward, like a math graph) matter for a course, not just a trivia fact?

Because every formula in Modules 11–13 that stacks elements vertically ("place the next block below the previous one") means adding to y, not subtracting. Get the direction backwards and every element renders in the mirror-opposite vertical order from what the DOM specifies —a real, diagnosable bug class, not just a coordinate curiosity.
